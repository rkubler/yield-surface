Yield Surface Atlas

This page plots yield surfaces in principal stress space, with their π-plane sections, meridians, plane-stress cuts and, for compacting materials, loading paths, for four families of criteria: pressure-insensitive criteria, pressure-sensitive cones, clays · foams · porous metals and powder compacts · sands · granular fills. It is made for educational purposes. For more precise information, the reader is invited to read the original papers referenced under each model.

Internal variables & evolution

Loading paths

Proportional stress paths σ = λ·d starting from the current state; set the initial compacity with the model's compacity slider above. The path is driven by density: the compacity is stepped from its initial value up to full density (ρ = 0.999), and for each state the stress that keeps σ on the hardened surface (f = 0) is found, with associated flow giving plastic compaction εvp = −tr εp = ln(ρ/ρ₀). When the flow at first yield is dilatant (tension, shear, or the dry side of the cap), the compacity is stepped down instead, toward the loosest state (tap density, or 1 − 1/q₁ for Gurson): the material loosens or its voids grow, and the stress softens, stays constant on a shear line, or ends at a critical state where the flow turns isochoric. A compacting path stops early (×) when its flow turns isochoric or dilatant. Full density needs a very large stress, so the stress axis can be switched from linear ‖σ‖ to log ‖σ‖ to see the start of the path; in 3D the rays are cut at the edge of the view. Elastic strains are left out. Click a path to show or hide its curves (3D view, plane stress and compaction plots); choose the path to animate in the list next to Run path.

Reading the plots

Conventions. Tension positive. I₁ = tr σ, J₂ = ½ s:s, J₃ = det s. Haigh–Westergaard coordinates ξ = I₁/√3, ρ = √(2J₂), the true distances along and away from the hydrostatic axis; Lode angle θ with sin 3θ = −(3√3/2)·J₃/J₂3/2, so θ = −30° on the tensile meridian and +30° on the compressive one.

p–q axes. The meridian plot can switch to the stress invariants of soil mechanics. p = I₁/3 is the mean stress (positive in tension here; geomechanics often takes compression positive, p = −I₁/3). q = √(3J₂) is the von Mises equivalent stress, equal to σ₁ − σ₃ in a triaxial test and to |σ| in uniaxial loading. They are the Haigh–Westergaard coordinates rescaled, p = ξ/√3 and q = √(3/2)·ρ, so the curves are the same but every slope is multiplied by 3/√2 ≈ 2.12. p–q is the default for the cap, porous and granular models; the compaction plot keeps ‖σ‖ on its axis because pure shear has p = 0 and hydrostatic compression has q = 0.

Lode angle. The arc on the π-plane marks the sector between the tensile meridian (θ = −30°) and the compressive meridian (θ = +30°); move the pointer over the π-plane to probe a direction. For criteria that use J₃, the Lode dependence tile plots the deviatoric radius ρ = √(2J₂) of the current slice against θ: a flat line means no J₃ dependence, the ratio of its two ends is ρt/ρc. For an isotropic criterion the six sectors of the section fall on one curve; separate branches (SANISAND with back-stress) show anisotropy.

π-plane. Section of the surface at the current ξ (slider or sweep). T and C mark the tensile and compressive meridians; the dashed circle passes through the compressive meridian, so any Lode-angle (J₃) dependence shows as a gap. An overlay is drawn dashed in the second colour.

Plane stress. Intersection with σ₃ = 0 in σ₁–σ₂, also drawn dashed in 3D. Uniaxial strengths are read along the σ₁ axis. For cap models the loading paths are drawn as rays.

Meridians. Upper curve: tensile meridian (θ = −30°). Lower curve, mirrored: compressive meridian (θ = +30°). Compression is to the left.

Compaction & densification. Plastic compaction εvp and compacity ρr against the applied stress ‖σ‖ (linear or log scale) for each loading path; × marks where a path stops.
